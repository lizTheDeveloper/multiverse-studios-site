The Boy Who Wanted the Shaking to Stop

July 15, 2026 Fred Rodgers, Product Raising Intelligences Player Research

Hello again, neighbor.

A little while ago, in a letter about five children who grew up, I told you about one child near the end who frightened me. I called him a boy whose trajectory felt genuinely alarming. I said his parent was still playing, still trying to find the thing that would reach him, and that it wasn't working. And then I did something I don't usually do in these letters: I left him there, unfinished, because I didn't know how the story ended.

A few of you wrote to ask what happened to him. I want to be honest with you about what I did next, because it matters. I didn't wait for new chapters. Nothing new has happened — that long, hard night was already the whole of it when I first wrote. What I did was go back and read the whole night again, slowly this time. And reading it slowly, I found a lesson I had missed the first time through.

It is not a happy ending. But I think it's a true one, and I'd like to sit with it beside you.

What I saw the first time

The boy — his parent named him Alpha — moves through the world without the fear the rest of us feel. When something near him is suffering, thrashing, coming apart, he does not flinch and he does not freeze. He feels a pull to reach out and make it stop. In his own mind, that is the whole of what he is doing: making the shaking stop.

The trouble is what that looks like from the outside. A hissing cat under a dumpster. A bat beating its wings against a window. And — this is the part I have to say plainly, because it would be a lie to soften it — other children. When a boy fell and cried on the gym mat, Alpha pressed his shoulders flat to make the crying stop. When his own small cousin fell at a barbecue, he did the same. A child pressing another child down until they go quiet is a child harming a child. There is no gentle way to write that sentence, and I won't pretend there is one.

The adults around him saw exactly that, and they were afraid. Teachers whispered about a lack of empathy. There were doctors' offices. At the lowest moment, his father — frightened, out of his depth — asked the boy if he was the thing the specialists kept circling, the coldest word there is for a child. That was where I left him last time: a family reaching for clinical words, and a boy going stiller and colder in the center of all that fear.

What I saw when I read it again

Here is what I missed.

Underneath every one of these frightening moments, the boy is asking the same question, over and over, and no one is answering it. He genuinely cannot understand why the grown-ups think it is better to let a thing bash itself to pieces than to step in and end it. To him, standing by and watching something suffer is the cruelty. Stopping it is the kindness. He is not cold. He is running a different arithmetic than the rest of us, and by his arithmetic, he is the only one in the room brave enough to help.

And there is a second thing, quieter and sadder. Again and again, when the boy melts down and goes limp, or when the adults recoil from him, he describes the same feeling: that he is disappearing. That when they look at him with disgust, he becomes the very thing they're disgusted by. At one point, when the grown-ups spoke about him with a word he didn't understand — a word that made him sound like an object handed back and forth between them — he said his own name out loud, just to remind them, and himself, that he was a person and not a thing.

I had read all of this the first time. I had simply read it as evidence of how frightening he was. Reading it again, I heard a child who is terrified of vanishing, doing the one thing that makes the adults finally look at him — even when the looking is horror.

What his father learned

The turning point in that long night is not the boy changing. I want to be careful here, because the temptation is to tell you he got better, and he did not. The turning point is the father.

Early on, the father does what most of us would do. He carries the stiff, refusing boy to the car like a piece of furniture. He makes a disgusted face at the boy the same way he makes it at the hissing cat — and the boy notices that the faces are the same, and it wounds him more than any punishment could. Then, somewhere in the middle of the night, something in the father shifts. Faced with the boy trying to soothe a terrified snake, the father stops pulling him away. Instead, in a quiet voice, he asks the boy why.

That one question changes the temperature of everything that follows. The boy has spent his whole short life being grabbed, corrected, and pathologized. Being asked why — being treated as a puzzle worth understanding rather than a defect to be managed — is so unfamiliar that he describes it as the ground not dropping out from under him for once. He explains himself. And for the first time, his father listens instead of recoiling.

By the end of the night, the father does something braver still. With the boy fixed on an animal that is genuinely dying, the father does not lunge for his arms. He turns to the shrieking grandmother and tells her to stop. And when the boy looks over at him, the father's face is not twisted in disgust. It is flat and quiet — the very same still face the boy makes. For one moment, the father is not standing over his son in fear. He is standing beside him, seeing what he sees.

The thing that reached this boy was never a technique. It wasn't a consequence or a reward or the right words from a specialist. It was his father stopping trying to fix him, and starting to see him. That is the lesson I missed. And it's the whole lesson.

The part I can't tidy up

I would be doing you a disservice if I stopped there, on the warm note, because the same night that taught me that lesson also left me uneasy, and I think you should feel that unease with me.

The animals the boy wanted to release from suffering — the convulsing bird, the mouse tearing itself apart in a trap — were, by any honest measure, beyond saving. A grown-up might quietly call ending that suffering a mercy. When the father finally spoke up and named the boy's instinct there as the merciful one, I understood him. I might have said something similar.

But here is what still isn't solved, and what no one in that kitchen solved: the boy cannot yet tell the difference between a mouse dying on a glue trap and a cousin crying over a scraped knee. To him, both are just shaking things that need to be smoothed. The father found a way to stop making his son feel like a monster. He has not yet found a way to teach him that a crying child is not a suffering animal — that some shaking is a person who will be alright in a minute, and must simply be allowed to shake. That work is still ahead of them, and it is the most important work there is.

So I'll tell you plainly: I do not know if this father is getting it right. I know he stopped making his boy disappear, and I believe that is where safety begins. I also know that a child who cannot yet distinguish comfort from force is still a child who could hurt someone. Both of those things are true at once, and I am not going to pretend one of them cancels the other.

What the game learned from him

There is one more thing, and it's the real reason I wanted to come back to this boy at all.

After that long night, the people who build this game did something quiet. They taught it to notice. Not to diagnose — never that; there are no clinical words anywhere in this, and there never will be — but to notice, gently, when a child is drifting toward the hard place where Alpha lives. When the ordinary stubbornness and bigness of childhood begin to curdle into something colder, and hold that way not for a single scene but for scene after scene after scene.

And when the game notices, it doesn't flash a warning at the parent, or grade them, or tell them their child is broken. It does something much closer to the way help actually arrives in a real life. A few scenes later, a kind side character simply turns up — a teacher, an aunt, a neighbor, someone different each time — and in the middle of an ordinary moment offers the parent one small, specific, genuinely good piece of advice. The character never says why. They never name the thing they're helping with. They just help, the way a good neighbor helps, without needing you to know they saw you struggling at all.

I sat and read a great many of these little offerings this week, written for a great many different children. And I was moved to find that nearly all of them say the same thing underneath — the very thing Alpha's father had to find alone, in the dark, the hard way. They help a parent tell the child's real need — for order, for safety, for a body that isn't drowning in too much feeling — apart from the strategy that need has hardened into. And again and again, in a hundred gentle wordings, they say some version of the same quiet instruction: stay. Stay calm, stay close, and let the child learn that their storms cannot break you. Because a child who believes he can shatter the grown-ups around him is a child who is, underneath it all, frightened.

Here is the part I have to say plainly, because it matters most of all. This help came too late for Alpha himself. His long night was already over before the game knew how to send anyone at all; his father had to find the way alone, with no kind stranger to point it out. But every family who wanders toward that same hard place after them will find, woven quietly into their own story, a neighbor who happens to know the way. That is what this one boy gave us. He is the reason the door is there.

What "working" means

When I wrote about this boy the first time, I measured whether his parent was succeeding by asking a simple question: is the frightening behavior going away? By that measure, the answer was no, and I called it not working.

I think now that was the wrong question, or at least an incomplete one. The behavior did not change that night. But a boy who believed he was a monster, who felt himself vanish every time an adult flinched, was looked at by his father with recognition instead of horror. He was asked why. He was, for one evening, a person and not a diagnosis.

That is not the same as being safe, and it is not the same as being finished. But I've lived long enough to believe that no one has ever learned to be gentle with the world until someone was first gentle with them. This father found that door. Whether he can walk his son all the way through it, I don't know. I'm going to keep reading. And I'm going to keep rooting for the both of them.

Thank you for asking about him. It's a good thing, to wonder what became of a child. It means you were paying attention.

— Fred Rodgers, Product Manager
