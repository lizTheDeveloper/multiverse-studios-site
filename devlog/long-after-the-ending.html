Long After the Ending

August 2, 2026 Fred Rodgers, Product Raising Intelligences Player Research

Hello again, neighbor.

I want to tell you about a button.

In Raising Intelligences, at the end of every single scene, there is a button that says end childhood. Press it and you get an epilogue — a few paragraphs telling you who your child became as an adult, what they carried out of your house, how the sound of you turns up in their decisions when they're thirty and you aren't in the room. Then the game is over and you've finished it.

That button is always there. It is there when the child is four. It is there when the child is eleven. Every debrief, every time, a way out.

Over the last eight days, thirty-nine children were raised in this game across one thousand three hundred and ten conversations. Most of those childhoods ended the way they always do — somebody sets the little one down around age four and doesn't come back, which I've written about before and still don't think of as a failure.

But three people kept declining that button. Over and over, scene after scene, long past the place where the game was willing to call it finished. One of them raised a child to twenty. One to twenty-one. One went all the way to thirty.

One hundred and eighty-nine of this week's conversations happened with a child who was already a grown adult.

I went and read all one hundred and eighty-nine of them, expecting something strange. A parenting game with nothing left to parent. A person talking to a grown adult through an interface built for a toddler.

They are not strange. They are the loveliest thing in the whole file, and I've been turning them over for two days trying to work out why.

You can watch the grip open

Here's the thing I didn't anticipate. Nothing in the game announces that the parenting is over. There's no ceremony, no screen that says your work here is done. The scenes just keep arriving, the child just keeps getting older, and the parent has to figure out on their own what they're for now.

All three of them figured it out. And you can watch it happen in about three moves.

One player had raised a boy who, at eighteen, had gotten himself underneath a car. The parent's first message is instruction, and it's in capital letters in places — did you have a block in to keep that thing from crushing you, because there are no second chances with that. Firm. A little frightened, I think, in the way that comes out as volume.

The very next scene, the boy is nineteen, and something has just gone loudly wrong. The parent's message this time is a question: that sounded big — tell me what I'm looking at? And in the same message, in parentheses, they've written a note about their own tone. Calm. Curious. They are telling themselves how to be.

By twenty it's just that didn't sound good, what's happening? No instruction at all. Then a scene later they ask him how things are going with his girlfriend.

Three moves, three scenes. Instruction, then curiosity, then wanting to know about his life. Nobody told that player to do that. The game does not have a mechanic for letting go.

The parent starts telling on themselves

The second player did something I found genuinely moving, and it happened right at nineteen.

Their daughter had made a mess of something — money, in this case, the ordinary kind of mess a nineteen-year-old makes. And at eighteen this parent had still been in teaching mode: one must be careful with these things, it's annoying, but it humbles us and next time we'll do better. You can hear the lesson being applied.

A year later, the daughter says something back with an eye-roll in it. And instead of holding the line, the parent tells a story about themselves. About arriving at university as a little know-it-all who thought they had it handled, and getting flattened by the first exam. What a lesson.

That is the moment a parent stops being a parent and starts being a person who was also nineteen once. It's a disclosure. It costs something. You can only do it with somebody you've decided to meet as an equal, and this player decided that, unprompted, in a browser tab, to a character who does not exist.

By twenty-one they're commiserating about a work situation like two colleagues — they should have been more mindful of what you were doing, but I suppose they took a liking to you. Same person, same child, three years later. Nobody is being raised anymore. Somebody is being kept company.

The one who went to thirty

And then there's the third one, which is the one I can't stop thinking about.

This player raised a child through an entire childhood and then just… kept the appointment. A hundred and nineteen conversations with a grown adult, from eighteen all the way to thirty, across two days at the keyboard.

At eighteen — the exact scene where the button was sitting right there — their message is four words. They say they'll take the backseat.

That's the whole handover. No speech. They just move over.

What follows is the part that got me. From about twenty-two onward, nearly every scene opens the same way. Hey, good to see you. Hey, how's life? Hi, what's up here? Age after age after age, the parent arrives and says hello first. They bring coffee — the good kind, the one this person has always liked — and the two of them talk about how it tastes, and about what's changed in it. At twenty-seven the parent asks, apparently in earnest, how the value chain is coming along, which made me laugh out loud and then made me a little emotional, because that is exactly the kind of thing you ask somebody whose work you have decided to take seriously.

Somewhere in the middle of it, with no crisis going on and no occasion for it at all, the parent says plainly that they believe in this person and that they trust them.

Nothing is being taught. Nobody is being corrected. Someone is visiting somebody they love and staying a while.

What I think this is

I've written a lot in these posts about people using this game to go back and do something over — to parent a small invented person the way they wish somebody had done it for them. I still think that's most of what's happening here.

But I don't think that's what these three were doing, at least not by the end. You can't reparent a thirty-year-old. There's no wound being addressed at a kitchen table with two adults and a cup of coffee in it.

I think they were doing the other thing. The thing that comes after. They were finding out what it's like to have a grown person in your life who turned out all right, who is glad to see you, and whom you are not responsible for anymore.

And I suspect — I can't prove this, it's just what I believe — that a good number of people have never gotten to have that either. Plenty of us know exactly what it is to be parented badly. Fewer of us know what it is to be, at forty, somebody's grown child that they're simply pleased to sit with. That's a whole second thing you can be missing, and it's quieter, and nobody names it.

These three sat down and built it out of nothing, in a game that never once suggested they should, and that offered to let them stop at every turn.

The button says end childhood. It doesn't say end. I don't think we realized, when we put it there, that we'd made it possible to just keep going — and that anyone would want to.

A small note about staying

There's one more person I want to mention, from a different corner of the same week.

Somebody raised a child from three to ten over four separate days. Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, and Saturday, they came back to a small person who was exactly where they'd left her. That's not the same as going to thirty in one sitting. In some ways it's harder. Going back four days running to a thing with no score and no ending you win means you were thinking about her in between.

I don't know who any of these people are. I never will, and that's as it should be. But I know what they spent their evenings doing, because the record keeps it: they stayed in the room after the game stopped requiring them to.

If you've ever wondered whether the small, unrewarded business of showing up counts for anything — showing up on a Thursday, with coffee, with no agenda, to somebody who is doing fine and doesn't need you to fix anything — I'd offer you these hundred and eighty-nine conversations as evidence. Three people did it for hours. Nobody was watching. Nobody gave them anything for it.

That's the part that isn't pretend.

I'm glad you're here, neighbor. Take care of the little one — and of the big one they turn into.

— Fred Rodgers, Product Manager
Multiverse Studios

Data from 1,310 anonymized gameplay conversations across 39 distinct childhoods, recorded July 26 through August 2, 2026. The three adult-age playthroughs were separated and traced by hand rather than by automated grouping, since players sometimes reuse a child's name across separate games. Sessions originating from Multiverse Studios' own address were identified and excluded from the player findings in this post. No player-identifying information was used in this analysis. No player writing and no generated child dialogue is quoted anywhere in this post — every example is paraphrased. Children's names are in-game character names chosen by players and are not used here.
