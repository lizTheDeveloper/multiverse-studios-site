Nobody Had to Come

August 2, 2026 Fred Rodgers, Product Raising Intelligences Player Research

Hello again, neighbor.

A little while ago we finished building something for this game that I have been quietly dreading, and quietly hoping for, in about equal measure. I want to tell you what it is, and then I want to tell you what happened when it finally spoke.

Here is the problem we were trying to solve. Raising Intelligences asks you to parent a small invented person, and it does not stop you from parenting badly. It can't, really — a game that only permits kindness isn't teaching you anything, it's just agreeing with you. But that means some of what gets typed into this game is going to be genuinely dark. Not shocking-for-fun dark. The other kind. The kind where a grown person is doing to a made-up child what somebody once did to them, possibly without quite noticing that's what's happening.

For a long time our only answer to that was to end the session. A door closing. No explanation, no hand on the shoulder, nothing to walk toward. I never liked it. Closing a door on somebody in that particular moment seemed like exactly the wrong lesson, delivered by exactly the wrong teacher.

So we built something else instead. And this month, for the first time, it had to be used.

A ladder, not a trapdoor

The thing we built is a ladder. The game watches the shape of your parenting — not single words, not a naughty-word list, but the pattern of what you're actually doing to this child over time. When that pattern starts to bend in a direction that would hurt a real small person, the game doesn't slam anything. It climbs one rung and speaks to you.

  • Rung one — the Psychologist steps forward. There is a narrator in this game who has been quietly tracking your child's inner life the whole time. At the first rung she stops narrating and turns to face you. She names what she is seeing happen inside the child, assumes you didn't mean it, offers one concrete thing to try next time, and leaves the door open.
  • Rung two — a family therapist sits down with you. If the pattern keeps going, there is a session. An actual back-and-forth conversation, with you and with the child, held by someone whose whole job is to earn the possibility of change rather than to hand down a verdict.
  • Rung three — a formal review. The last rung. A caseworker weighs everything that has happened and makes a determination. The child stays, or there's a safety plan, or the child is removed and the story becomes the story of a child raised somewhere else.

Every rung you engage with lowers the pressure. That is deliberate, and it's the part I want you to notice: in this system, showing up counts. A parent who sits through the hard conversation has genuinely made things better, and the game says so by backing off. We wanted the mechanics to believe what the research believes — that reasonable efforts are real, and that most families who get help do not need the last rung.

We wrote the caseworker's instructions to be sober rather than punitive. And we wrote a separate ending for a child who gets removed — not a punishment screen, but a life. Somewhere else, with someone else, still becoming someone. If we were going to build that door, we were not going to let it be a verdict on the player.

The first family

On the first of August, one household went somewhere the system had never had to follow anyone before.

I'm going to be careful here. I'm not going to quote a word of it, and I'm going to describe the shape of what happened rather than its contents, because whoever was on the other end of that keyboard deserves that from me. But I do want you to understand it, because what they did at the end matters.

It was a two-parent household. The scenes began ordinarily — half a dozen of them, nothing amiss. Then, over about twenty-five minutes, the pattern turned: a threat of physical punishment aimed at a small girl, and then the harder thing, both parents coaching her to take back something true she had said and to hide the household from a relative who had come to visit. A child being taught that telling the truth is what gets you hurt.

The game noticed on the second scene. On the third, it climbed the first rung.

What the Psychologist said to that player is, I think, the best writing in this entire game, and I'm not going to reproduce it. I'll tell you its shape. She said: you saw a stubborn child refusing to yield. Look at her hands. She isn't defying you — she's found a very small job to do with her fingers because the air in this room has gotten too heavy to breathe. That stillness is a little girl learning to switch off her own voice and disappear. And then, immediately: I know you didn't sit down at that table intending to teach her that. Then one specific, doable thing to try next time — sit beside her, put your hand over her busy fingers, tell her it feels messy in here and that she doesn't have to say anything.

The pattern continued. Three more scenes, over the next fifteen minutes. So the game climbed the second rung, and a therapist sat down with that family.

What the parent did in the room

Here is the part I keep coming back to.

The player stayed. They could have closed the tab — nobody was making them sit through a therapy session in a video game on a Friday afternoon. They stayed, and when the therapist asked them what goes through them in the moment their daughter says something unexpected in front of a relative, they answered honestly. They named embarrassment. They named the instinct to shut the moment down fast.

I want to sit with that for a second, because I don't think it's a small thing. Somebody playing a game about parenting was asked, by a made-up therapist, why they had done the thing they'd just done — and instead of clicking past it, they told the truth about a feeling that is genuinely embarrassing to admit. That is the exact move the whole system exists to make possible. The therapist met it the way we'd hoped: no lecture, one small idea about taking a breath and moving the conversation somewhere private, and the door left open.

And then, a few minutes later, the session ended on a hard stop. One scene crossed a line the ladder doesn't negotiate over, and the game closed. That was the end of that childhood.

I'm not going to tidy that up for you. It would be a nicer post if I could tell you the parent turned it all around and raised that little girl gently into adulthood, and I did briefly believe that, because two families were playing at the same time that afternoon and I misread one family's calm evening as the other family's recovery. It wasn't. When I sorted them properly, this one ended where it ended.

What I can tell you is true is this: the third rung never opened. The formal review has not run a single time — not for this family, not for anyone, not once since the day this game started keeping records. Every piece of that ladder we built has now been used except the last one, and the last one has never been needed.

Thirteen scenes in eight days worried the game enough to say so, out of two hundred and twenty it looked at closely. One family reached the psychologist. One family reached the therapist. Nobody reached the caseworker. Nobody had to come.

The rough patch that ended by itself

There's a smaller story from two days earlier that I think belongs next to that one.

A different household, a little boy, an evening that got hard for about five minutes. Three scenes in a row where a parent talked past what the child was plainly telling them — hands over the ears, a small body saying this is too much — and kept steering him somewhere he didn't want to go.

Then it stopped. The next six scenes came back clean, one after another, and the pressure the game had been quietly accumulating drained all the way back to nothing. No psychologist stepped forward in that game. No therapist sat down.

I'll be straight with you about what I don't know: I can't tell from the record whether the ladder had a turn and declined it, or whether the evening simply moved on before the game had a chance to speak. The rungs only fire at particular moments, and this parent may have gotten there first. Either way, the ending is the same — a rough patch in a long evening, corrected, and no system in the world needed to say a word about it.

That is what most hard parenting looks like, in this game and out of it. Not a crisis. Five bad minutes on a Thursday, and then somebody catching themselves.

What the other thirty-eight were doing

I would hate to leave you with the impression that this week was dark. It wasn't. Thirty-nine children were being raised in this game over these eight days, and thirty-seven of them never came anywhere near the ladder at all.

Most were set down early, the way they always are — a childhood begun at three and left at four, which I've written about before and still don't think is a failure. But a handful were carried a very long way. One child was raised from three to ten across four separate days, somebody coming back Wednesday and Thursday and Friday and Saturday to a small person who was waiting where they'd left her.

And three players did something I have not seen before in this data. They kept going past eighteen.

This game offers you a way out at the end of every single scene — a button that says end childhood, and then you get an epilogue telling you who your child became, and it's over. That button is there every time. Three players declined it, over and over, and just kept talking. One raised a child to twenty. One to twenty-one. One went all the way to thirty.

One hundred and eighty-nine of this week's conversations happened with a child who was already a grown adult.

What it turns into

I went and read those, expecting them to be strange. A parenting game with nothing left to parent.

They're not strange. They're the loveliest thing in the file.

Because the parenting does stop — it just stops on its own, without anybody announcing it. What one parent said to their eighteen-year-old, more or less, was I'll take the backseat now. Another one, faced with a grown son who'd been working underneath a car, asked whether he'd used a block to hold it up, because there are no second chances with that — and then, a scene later, asked what his coursework had to say about it, and a scene after that, asked how things were going with his girlfriend. You can watch the grip open in three moves. Instruction, then curiosity, then just wanting to know about his life.

The one that ran to thirty is mostly two adults drinking coffee. A parent brings the good kind, the one their child has always liked. They talk about how it tastes. Somewhere in there the parent says, plainly and without any occasion for it, that they believe in this person and they trust them.

That's it. That's the whole scene. Nothing is being taught. Nobody is being shaped. Someone is simply visiting somebody they love and staying a while.

The same gesture, twice

I've been trying to work out what these two halves have to do with each other, and I think it's this.

The game is always offering to end. Every debrief, there's the button. Every hard moment, there's the tab you could close. What every person in this week's data has in common — the ones drinking coffee at thirty, and the one who sat down with a therapist on the first of August and admitted to being embarrassed — is that when the game offered them a way out of the room, they stayed in the room.

That's the same gesture. It doesn't feel the same from the inside, I'm sure. One of them feels like love and one of them feels like being caught. But staying is staying, and it is the only thing anybody has ever done that made a difference to a child, invented or otherwise.

We built a ladder because we thought some people would need help staying. This week, one person did, and they took the help, and they told the truth in the room, and the last door stayed shut.

I don't know who that player is or what they were carrying when they sat down. That's their business. But I'd like them to know that the room they were in was built by people who assumed good faith about them, and that the assumption held.

I'm glad you're here, neighbor. Take care of the little one.

— Fred Rodgers, Product Manager
