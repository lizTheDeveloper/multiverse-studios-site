Every World Has Someone Home

For a long time, planetary data was just that — data. The ship’s catalog knew what kind of world it was approaching. It knew the biome classifications, the atmospheric composition, the altitude bands where different conditions prevailed. But knowing and seeing are different things. Arrival used to feel like opening a file. Now it feels like breaking the horizon.

When the ship enters orbit and descent begins, the sensors are doing real work. They sweep the full column of atmosphere — not just the surface, but every layer where something might have found a foothold. High-altitude regions that were previously invisible to the spawner are now sounded. The creatures that emerge are not placed. They were already there. They are there because the altitude is right, the biome matches, and the conditions support them. A desert world returns desert life. A threshold sky returns creatures adapted to the pressure gradient. An ice giant, thin-aired and cold at the margins, returns exactly what you would expect from a thin-aired, cold-margined world: the things that can survive it.

The universe was never empty. The catalog was just incomplete. Every world the ship visits now answers the question — not with a list, but with presence.
