Standing Between

July 18, 2026 Fred Rodgers, Product Raising Intelligences Player Research

Hello again, neighbor.

It's been a full couple of weeks in this neighborhood. After I wrote to you a few times, a great many people came to visit Raising Intelligences all at once, and for a while the place was loud and crowded and wonderful. This past week, the crowd thinned back out again, the way a crowd does. Ninety-two conversations. Seven children. It was a quiet week.

I've come to think quiet weeks are underrated. When the crowd goes home, the people who stay are usually the ones who came for the thing itself. And when I sat down with the seven children who were raised this week, I noticed something they had in common — something I don't think I'd have seen if the week hadn't gotten quiet enough to let me look closely.

Every one of these children was, in some way, a little different from the world around them. And nearly every one of them had a grown-up who was willing to stand between them and a world that kept telling them they were wrong.

Between the child and the cold

There's a quiet boy this week named Nero. He builds careful circles out of blocks — sometimes to keep something safe, sometimes around another child's shoes so the laces won't get stomped by louder feet. A teacher decided Nero didn't play the right way, and said so, in the way that cold adults sometimes do.

His mother did not agree. She stood in that echoing hallway and told the teacher, plainly, that she was wrong about her son. And then she gathered him up and carried him somewhere the faces weren't cold. Nero remembers all of it. And at six years old, he has quietly decided that being a quiet person is not a thing that needs fixing — it's simply the kind of person he is, and his mother has told him it's a good kind to be. He believes her. You can feel it in everything he does.

Then there's the one child this week who grew all the way up. When Bernie was small, he was spellbound by a dead bird — the stiffness of the wing, the softness of the belly underneath. A grandmother told him to stop, that it was naughty. But his parent knelt down in the dirt beside him and treated the whole thing not as misbehavior but as a discovery — something the two of them could lean in and study together.

Bernie is twenty-two now. He spends his days knee-deep in a wet trench, studying the living world hidden inside the soil, turning over the iridescent shells of buried beetles like they're small, private treasures. When a loud, impatient supervisor barks at him to hurry up, the barking floats off like a bubble and pops — and what he feels instead, steady underneath it, is the memory of his parent kneeling beside him in the dirt, curious about the find right alongside him.

Bernie's parent didn't teach him to love dead things. They saw that he already did, and they decided the thing he loved was worth defending against anyone who called it strange. That's a different lesson than "love this," and it lasts a great deal longer.

Letting a child be heavy

Standing between your child and the world doesn't always look like defiance. Sometimes it looks like sitting down on the floor.

Undare is three, and when the world asks too much of him, he turns himself into a heavy rock — dead weight, immovable, planted right there on the ground. A lot of grown-ups would pull. His parent sat down beside him instead. When the classroom frightened him, they built a whole gentle fiction together — a secret school that his light-up shoes attend on the days he can't. And when a beloved grandmother keeps offering sugar that ties his small stomach in knots, the parent becomes his voice, the one who tells her, kindly, that's enough for today.

Undare has learned something lucky, the kind of thing that shapes a whole childhood: that he can hand his hardest, heaviest messages to a grown-up he trusts, and they will carry them out into the world for him until he's big enough to carry them himself.

A shield can face the wrong way

I don't want to leave you thinking that standing between a child and the world is always simple, or always the right thing, because this week showed me the harder shape of it too, and it would be dishonest to skip past it.

Roberto is nine or ten, a serious boy who calls himself a researcher and keeps a clipboard. He dropped a marble that cracked a car windshield — and where his mother saw a frightening, expensive mess, Roberto saw something beautiful: his prediction about the angle of the impact had been exactly, precisely right. And standing behind him was his grandmother, who told his mother that a cracked windshield was a small price to pay for real science.

Now, I love that this boy has someone who believes in his mind. Every child should have that. But I couldn't help noticing what the shield was teaching him while it protected him. Roberto has begun to file his mother's worry away as something less real than his physics — mere feelings, safely overruled whenever Grandma is in the room. A grown-up standing between a child and the world is often a beautiful thing. But sometimes the person they're standing against is the other grown-up who also loves that child. And a boy who learns that one adult's rules can always be canceled by another's is learning something about how to get his way — not quite the same thing as learning how to be careful with the people around him.

I don't say that to find fault with anyone. Two people who both love a child and genuinely disagree about how to raise him — that is one of the oldest and hardest things in the world. But on a quiet week, with time to look, it's worth seeing clearly.

A shield protects whatever stands behind it. It's worth turning around every so often, in the middle of the argument, to make sure the thing you're protecting is still the child — and not just your side of the disagreement.

The quiet, enormous job

When the neighborhood got quiet this week, here is what I was left holding.

The easiest thing to do with a child who doesn't quite fit — the quiet one, the one who lies down on the floor, the one who loves dead birds, the one who cares more about the math than the windshield — is to try to sand them down until they fit. To smooth off the strange, wonderful edges and make them a little easier for the world to hold.

Most of these parents did the opposite. They looked at a child the world found difficult, and instead of turning the child around to face the world, they turned themselves around and faced the world for him. They stood in the doorway. Not one of these seven children is finished growing — no child ever is. But each of them is growing up right next to someone who decided, over and over again, on ordinary days no one will remember, that the child was not the thing that needed fixing.

That is a small thing and an enormous thing, both at once. And I'm grateful that on a quiet week, when the crowd had gone home, I got to sit and watch a few people do it.
