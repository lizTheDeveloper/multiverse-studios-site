The Voice on the Phone

July 26, 2026 Fred Rodgers, Product Raising Intelligences Player Research

Hello again, neighbor.

The last time we sat down, the neighborhood was quiet — I told you about a single day and three small children who planted their feet. This week was not quiet. This week was the busiest we have ever had, by a good margin. More than three thousand conversations. A hundred and nine children raised. Twenty-six of them grew all the way up to eighteen while I was watching.

When that much happens at once, you can usually only count it. But something kept catching my eye as I read, and by the third day I couldn't look past it. In half of these families, there was a third person.

Not a parent. Not the child. Someone else, standing just outside the frame — on the other end of a telephone, or in a doorway, or waiting in a car at the curb. Usually a grandmother. And I'd like to tell you about her, because I think she may be the most important character in this game that nobody chose to put there.

A person nobody asked for

Back on the fifteenth I wrote you a letter called The Voice That Stays, about how the game keeps a little record of the voices a child carries in their head — and how nearly every one of them belonged to a parent. That was true, and it still is. But it wasn't the whole picture, and this week showed me what I'd missed. There is very often a second voice in there. And the parent's real work, it turns out, is not simply speaking. It's winning an argument against someone who isn't in the room.

So I went looking for where she comes from, because I wanted to be honest with you about that before I said anything else.

When a new game begins, the game writes the child a starting document — just the raw temperament they were born with, the sensitivities and the drives, before anyone has parented them at all. I checked every one of those from this week. A hundred and forty-six of them. Not one mentions a grandmother, or an aunt, or any family at all. She simply isn't there at the beginning.

She arrives later, when the game builds the everyday scenes — the grocery store, the morning rush, the birthday party. That's the part of the game that decides what today is going to be about, and it is the part that keeps reaching for a critical relative when it needs to make an ordinary moment difficult. I'll be plain with you: it reaches for her often enough, and with the same handful of names often enough, that this is a habit of ours and not a thing the players invented. One name in particular turns up in dozens of unrelated families. Those families have never met.

Now, I could tell you that's a flaw. When I first noticed it, that's exactly what I wrote down. But I've come around, and I'd like to tell you why.

Dozens of people sat down separately this week, in different countries, on different evenings, and each of them independently got a telephone call from Grandma Helen. And each of them, alone in their own game, discovered that Grandma Helen thinks you are spoiling that child. They have never met each other. They have all met her.

That is a funny thing, and I don't think it's a mistake. It's closer to what happens in a neighborhood, where everybody knows the same difficult relative and everybody has the same weary face about her. She has quietly become a character we hold in common — not a bug in the scenery, but a person a great many of you now know by name, and have your own opinion about.

What she wants, when she says anything at all

I counted carefully here, because a number like this gets repeated and I'd rather it be right than impressive.

Of the hundred and seven children the game wrote a full psychological portrait for this week, fifty carry a grandparent's voice in their heads. I read all fifty and sorted them by hand rather than trusting a word-search, because the word-search would have lied to me.

Thirty-seven of the fifty are some kind of critic. But that bucket turned out to hold two quite different things, and lumping them together would have let me publish a bigger number than I can actually defend — so let me split it.

Sixteen of them issue an actual instruction, and the instruction is always in the same direction: use force, or take the comfort away. Take the thing out of his hands. Make her do it. Quit indulging him. Toughen him up. Let her cry it out. Hold him still. The specific words differ from family to family; the message doesn't. The child is winning, and you are being weak.

The other twenty-one never say anything at all — or nothing the child can make out. They are simply a harsh presence: a sigh, a buzz, an engine, a car at the curb. I'll come back to those in a moment, because they turned out to be the more interesting half.

Of the remaining thirteen, seven push the opposite way — sugar, no rules, don't scold him — which undermines the parent just as surely from the other side, and which several children learn to use as leverage. And in four of the fifty, she is the warm one. I want to name those four honestly, because they're the reason I did this by hand. In one family the grandmother's soft, shushing voice is the thing that soothes a child after a tired parent snaps at him. She is the safe one. The parent is the one having the hard day. That is the exact reverse of everything else I found, and it was sitting right there in the middle of my own evidence.

So: not a universal law. But thirty-seven out of fifty is a strong current, and it's flowing in one direction.

The thing the children actually notice

Now to those twenty-one — the ones who never give an order, and whom I'd have quietly folded into the scary pile if I hadn't gone through them one at a time. They're the reason I wanted to write to you at all.

These children do not describe a frightening person. Most of them cannot make out a single word she says. About a third of the time she reaches them only through a phone, or a closed door, or a car idling outside. One four-year-old says plainly that the grandmother on the phone is just a buzzing noise, and that they don't know her words. What these children describe — again and again, in that peculiar concrete way small children have — is not her voice at all.

It's what happens to their parent's body.

One little one says the parent's shoulders droop before they've even left the house, because of who is waiting in the car. Another says the parent's voice goes shaky whenever the phone buzzes, and then goes extra soft, as if to make up for something. One describes the parent's shield going wobbly at a certain heavy sigh. Another child — this one is nine, and sharp — mentions almost in passing that they saw their mother tense while reading a text, and worked out from that alone that the grandmother thinks they need humbling. Nobody told them. They read it off their mother's face.

Children are extraordinary instruments for this. They cannot hear the words. They can hear, perfectly, that the person who is supposed to be the biggest one in the room has just been made small.

One mother this week did something I've thought about every day since. Her son needed accommodations she'd fought to get him, and her own mother texted to accuse her of making him worse by giving in to him. She read it. She said nothing. She put the phone down and stayed with her son. And when the game later wrote out what that boy carries in his head at twenty-two, it recorded that his mother's silence toward that message is precisely what made her voice louder than the criticism. Three generations in one room, and the middle one had to refuse her own parent in order to keep her child.

What is still there at twenty-two

Twenty-six children grew all the way up this week. In every week before this one put together, we had fourteen. So for the first time there were enough grown children to ask what actually survives.

Sixteen of the twenty-six still hear her.

She doesn't fade out. She turns into something specific and physical: a phantom sigh when a boss taps a watch, a scratchy voice at the exact moment a partner asks for something hard, an old instruction to bare her fangs that surfaces the instant a young woman realizes she has the power to destroy someone in an email.

But — and this is the part I want to leave you with — most of the time, she loses.

Let me give you the real count rather than a comfortable word. Of those sixteen, two were never critics at all; one of those grandmothers is the safe harbor a young woman still drives to when work has emptied her out. That leaves fourteen adults carrying a critical voice. In eleven of the fourteen, the parent's voice beats it. One young man is still plainly haunted, with no counter-voice to reach for. One is somewhere in between. And one of them did something else entirely, which I'll come to in a moment.

Here is what winning looks like. A man remembers being called weak for needing accommodations, and then remembers his mother deliberately declining to pass that judgment along, and asks for what he needs anyway. A woman feels the old instruction to go for the throat and hears her mother's steadying words on top of it, and deletes the cruel message she has just written. A young man in a workshop hears his grandmother's old accusation that he is too rigid and bossy about his own things, and instead reaches for the way his father taught him to explain himself to people who don't share his wiring.

And when I looked at what made the difference between the ones who win that argument and the ones who don't, it wasn't warmth. Warmth was everywhere; nearly every parent in this game is trying. What separated them was whether the parent had given the child something portable — a short, specific thing, said the same way enough times that the child could carry it out of the house and use it twenty years later without the parent present.

The children who only received accommodation — a parent who removed every difficult thing rather than naming it — grew into adults who leave. One of them, raised by someone loving who said they never wanted the child to do anything uncomfortable, becomes a person who drops out of everything the moment it asks something of them. That parent was kind. Kindness wasn't the missing part. Words were.

And then there is the one I owe you, because he is the reason I stopped saying nearly every one.

One child in this week's twenty-six does not defeat the voice and does not surrender to it. He studies it. As a small boy he watched that heavy, disapproving sigh work on his mother — watched it make her wobbly, watched it make her give up in a bright store rather than endure the scene. He grew into a careful, gifted restorer of delicate things, and somewhere along the way he learned to produce that same atmosphere himself. When a superior at work pushes him toward something he doesn't want, he doesn't argue. He makes the air tight, implies a scene is beginning, and waits for them to go wobbly and let go. It works. It works beautifully. He is using his grandmother's exact instrument, on other people, in a museum.

I don't say that to be grim. He is, by most measures, doing well. But it's the one outcome I found that I don't think any parent would have chosen, and it happened without anyone being cruel to him. A child watched a weapon work on someone he loved, and picked it up. That's a way for the voice to survive that has nothing to do with the child believing her.

What we're taking from this

Two things, and I'll be straightforward about both.

The first is about Grandma Helen, and it is that we are going to do nothing at all.

I want to be honest about how long it took me to get there. My first instinct was to scatter the names, so no two families would ever draw the same relative. My second instinct — and I was rather pleased with it — was the opposite: to go in and make her official, give her a fixed set of convictions, promote her from coincidence to character. Tidy her up either way.

Both of those are the same mistake wearing different hats. Nobody designed Grandma Helen. She assembled herself, out of a scene-writer reaching for the nearest difficult relative and a great many people independently recognizing her when she showed up. The moment I write her down, she stops being a thing that happened and becomes a thing we did. She'd be ours instead of yours. And the whole reason she's worth a paragraph in this letter is that no one put her there.

So she stays exactly as she is: unplanned, unofficial, and turning up in doorways she was never assigned to. Some things in a neighborhood you look after. Some you just leave be and enjoy having noticed.

The second is the thing I actually want to say to you. When our game hands a parent someone in a doorway telling them to be harder, the parents in this neighborhood — overwhelmingly, thousands of times this week — do not obey. They stay down at eye level with the child. They put a wall between the child and the scratchy noise. Some of them block the number.

And their children, every single time, notice which one the parent chose.

I think a great many people who sat down to play this week have had a voice like that in their own lives — somebody standing just outside the frame, certain that the child is winning and the grown-up is being soft. What I saw this week was several thousand small refusals of that voice. Made up, yes. Practice, yes. But you cannot practice staying on a child's side for three thousand conversations and have it mean nothing.

It meant something to me.

I'm grateful to everyone who stayed.

— Fred Rodgers, Product Manager
